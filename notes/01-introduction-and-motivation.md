## Four motivations

A spectrum is meant to solve related problems, not merely repackage a sequence of spaces.

1. **Stabilization:** Maps between spaces eventually exhibit patterns that survive repeated suspension.
2. **Cohomology:** Generalized cohomology theories have *representing spectra* (Brown representability).
3. **Infinite looping:** A representing spectrum packages a compatible infinite sequence of deloopings of a space.
4. **Stable algebra:** Spectra form the basic example of stable homotopy theory; the analogy with chain complexes becomes precise through stable categories and module spectra.

These explanations motivate the same category from different directions. The first concerns maps, the second functors, the third spaces, and the fourth the categorical operations on them.

## 1. Suspension and stabilization

Throughout, take \(X\) to be a pointed space, with base point \(*\).

The **reduced suspension** is

\[
\Sigma X := S^1\wedge X
=\frac{X\times[0,1]}{X\times\{0,1\}\,\cup\,\{*\}\times[0,1]}.
\]

In particular, \(\Sigma S^n\cong S^{n+1}\) (up to the standard identifications).

A pointed map \(f:S^{n+k}\to S^n\) suspends to

\[
\Sigma f:S^{n+k+1}\to S^{n+1},
\]

and hence gives a homomorphism

\[
\pi_{n+k}(S^n)\longrightarrow\pi_{n+k+1}(S^{n+1}).
\]

We want to know what remains once \(n\) increases without bound *while the excess dimension \(k\) stays fixed*.

Define the **\(k\)-th stable stem** (for \(k\geq0\)) by

\[
\pi_k^s(\mathbb S)
:=\underset{n\to\infty}{\operatorname{colim}}\,
\pi_{n+k}(S^n).
\]

Here the transition maps are suspension. A colimit is necessary in the definition, but the Freudenthal theorem tells us that a fixed stem reaches its stable value after finitely many steps.

### Freudenthal suspension theorem: the useful range

If \(X\) is \(r\)-connected (\(\pi_i(X)=0\) for \(0\leq i\leq r\)), the suspension homomorphism

\[
\pi_i(X)\to\pi_{i+1}(\Sigma X)
\]

is an isomorphism for \(i\leq 2r\) and a surjection for \(i=2r+1\), under the usual connectedness/CW hypotheses.

For \(X=S^n\), which is \((n-1)\)-connected, substitute \(i=n+k\):

\[
n+k\leq2(n-1)
\iff n\geq k+2.
\]

Thus the suspension homomorphism stabilizes for \(n\geq k+2\) when \(k\geq0\).

**Warning:** In an intermediate range, suspension need not be injective. The passage to a stable stem can discard genuinely unstable information.

### Two examples

- **Stem zero:** \(\pi_n(S^n)\cong\mathbb Z\) for \(n\geq1\), detected by degree. Consequently \(\pi_0^s(\mathbb S)\cong\mathbb Z\).
- **Stem one:** \(\pi_3(S^2)\cong\mathbb Z\), generated by the Hopf map \(\eta:S^3\to S^2\). Its first suspension lies in \(\pi_4(S^3)\cong\mathbb Z/2\). Repeated suspensions give
  \(\pi_1^s(\mathbb S)\cong\mathbb Z/2\).
  The first suspension maps \(\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\); this illustrates how stabilization loses information.

The stable stem is indexed by the **difference of dimensions**, not either dimension by itself.

## 2. A category in which suspension is invertible

One can first formalize stable mapping classes between finite pointed CW complexes:

\[
[X,Y]_{\mathrm{stable}}
=
\operatorname*{colim}_{j}
[\Sigma^jX,\Sigma^jY]_*.
\]

The Spanier–Whitehead construction also allows *formal desuspensions*. Rather than regard \(\Sigma\) as an operation that increases dimension forever, we want it to have an inverse in the stable category.

This is not literally true in the homotopy category of pointed spaces. For example, arbitrary pointed spaces need not be suspensions. It becomes true in the homotopy theory of **spectra**.

A first model of a spectrum has levels \(E_0,E_1,\ldots\), together with structure maps

\[
\sigma_n:\Sigma E_n\to E_{n+1}.
\]

The categorical suspension functor on spectra will become invertible *up to stable equivalence*. That qualification matters: a levelwise map of spaces and a map in the stable homotopy category are not interchangeable.

## 3. Why generalized cohomology demands spectra

Ordinary singular cohomology \(H^n(X;A)\) is only one example of a cohomology theory. Complex topological \(K\)-theory \(KU^n(X)\), real \(K\)-theory \(KO^n(X)\), and cobordism theories are others.

For a *reduced* generalized cohomology theory \(\widetilde E^*\), important requirements include:

- Invariance under homotopy equivalence.
- Suspension isomorphisms \(\widetilde E^n(X)\cong\widetilde E^{n+1}(\Sigma X)\).
- Exactness associated to a homotopy cofiber sequence.
- The wedge axiom: \(\widetilde E^n(\bigvee_\alpha X_\alpha)\cong\prod_\alpha\widetilde E^n(X_\alpha)\).

The dimension axiom for ordinary cohomology is **not** required.

**Brown representability** says (with the appropriate CW hypotheses) that there is a representing spectrum \(E\) such that

\[
\widetilde E^n(X)
\cong
[\Sigma^\infty X,\Sigma^n E]_{\mathrm{Ho(Sp)}}.
\]

The bracket on the right means *homotopy classes of maps of spectra*. This is more than a slogan: it converts a cohomology theory, a sequence of contravariant functors, into maps into a single structured object.

### Examples to keep separate

| Theory | Representing spectrum | Characteristic feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Singular cohomology with \(A\)-coefficients | \(HA\), Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum | \(\pi_0(HA)=A\), other homotopy groups vanish |
| Complex topological \(K\)-theory | \(KU\) | Bott periodicity of period 2 |
| Real topological \(K\)-theory | \(KO\) | Bott periodicity of period 8 |

Cobordism introduces Thom spectra, such as \(MO\). They illustrate why spectra are connected with the geometry of manifolds, not only cohomology calculations.

## 4. Infinite loop spaces

Suppose a cohomology theory is represented, degree by degree, by pointed spaces \(E_n\):

\[
\widetilde E^n(X)\cong[X,E_n]_*.
\]

Combine this with the suspension isomorphism and the suspension–loop adjunction:

\[
[X,E_n]_*
\cong \widetilde E^n(X)
\cong \widetilde E^{n+1}(\Sigma X)
\cong[\Sigma X,E_{n+1}]_*
\cong[X,\Omega E_{n+1}]_*.
\]

By representability, the spaces \(E_n\) and \(\Omega E_{n+1}\) are weakly equivalent. Such a sequence is an **\(\Omega\)-spectrum**.

Its zeroth level \(E_0\) can be looped and delooped indefinitely:

\[
E_0\simeq\Omega E_1\simeq\Omega^2E_2\simeq\cdots.
\]

A useful refinement: grouplike \(E_\infty\)-spaces correspond to **connective** spectra. The infinite loop space alone generally does *not* determine the negative homotopy groups of an arbitrary nonconnective spectrum.

## 5. Why these objects resemble chain complexes

In the homotopy theory of spectra:

- Suspension is an equivalence.
- Homotopy fiber and homotopy cofiber sequences agree up to shifts.
- Mapping groups are abelian in every degree.
- Stable cofiber sequences produce long exact sequences of homotopy groups.
- For a ring \(R\), the derived category of \(R\)-modules can be modeled by modules over the Eilenberg–Mac Lane ring spectrum \(HR\).

Thus chain complexes are an important *special case* of stable algebra, but spectra also detect phenomena (including the positive stable homotopy groups of spheres) that ordinary chain complexes do not.

## Questions and exercises

1. Work out carefully why the \(k=1\) suspension \(\pi_3(S^2)\to\pi_4(S^3)\) is not an isomorphism even though the limit stabilizes.
2. Prove that \(\widetilde H^n(S^m;A)\) equals \(A\) when \(n=m\) and vanishes otherwise. Then explain why \(HA\) represents this theory.
3. Why does the suspension isomorphism of a cohomology theory give **loop** maps \(E_n\to\Omega E_{n+1}\), rather than just arbitrary maps \(\Sigma E_n\to E_{n+1}\)?
4. Identify a property of cup products lost upon suspension, even though reduced cohomology groups shift.
5. Distinguish (a) a prespectrum, (b) an \(\Omega\)-spectrum, and (c) a stable equivalence. These are *not* synonyms.
