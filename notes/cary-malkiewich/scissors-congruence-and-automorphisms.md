## 1. From ordinary scissors congruence to a spectrum

Fix a geometry \(X\), typically Euclidean, hyperbolic, or spherical, and a subgroup \(G\le I(X)\) of its isometry group. Classical scissors congruence studies polytopes modulo two operations:

1. cut a polytope into finitely many pieces with disjoint interiors;
2. move pieces using elements of \(G\).

The abelian group generated by polytopes with these relations is the degree-zero object:

\[
K_0(\mathcal P_G^X).
\]

If \(Pt(X)^t\) denotes the polytope module with the action that literally moves a polytope by \(g\), then

\[
K_0(\mathcal P_G^X)\cong H_0(G;Pt(X)^t).
\]

Inna Zakharevich's assembler K-theory gives a spectrum

\[
K(\mathcal P_G^X),
\]

whose homotopy groups \(K_m(\mathcal P_G^X)\) are the higher scissors-congruence groups. Degree zero remembers cut-and-reassemble equivalence classes; higher degrees retain coherent families of the cut-and-reassemble operations themselves.

That conceptual shift is the main reason this material belongs in a stable-homotopy notebook: a classical geometry problem becomes a structured spectrum.

## 2. The polytopal Tits complex

Malkiewich packages the geometry into a based space \(PT(X)\), the **polytopal Tits complex**. Informally, it is built by taking the geometry and quotienting by the homotopy colimit of its proper subspaces. Its reduced homology is concentrated in one degree and recovers the polytope module.

This resembles the Solomon–Tits phenomenon for buildings: a complicated incidence complex has homology concentrated at the top. In Euclidean geometry the corresponding module is identified with an affine Steinberg module.

A useful mental model:

- the original polytope problem has many cuts and subspaces;
- the Tits-type complex records their incidence coherently;
- top homology is the additive polytope invariant;
- stabilizing this construction exposes the entire K-theory spectrum.

Flags of proper subspaces can be drawn as nested affine subspaces; apartments and their gluings are the combinatorial skeleton behind the stable object.

## 3. Main Thom-spectrum theorem

The tangent bundle \(TX\) descends far enough along the construction of \(PT(X)\) to define a virtual desuspension

\[
PT(X)^{-TX}.
\]

The isometry group acts, so one can take homotopy orbits. Malkiewich's main theorem is an equivalence

\[
K(\mathcal P_G^X)\simeq PT(X)^{-TX}_{hG}.
\]

This is much stronger than a homology calculation. It identifies the higher scissors-congruence theory with an explicit Thom spectrum.

For Euclidean and hyperbolic geometry the polytopal Tits complex can be replaced by the suspension of the ordinary Tits complex. The spherical case needs a reduced variant and is subtler because the analogous comparison is not simply an equivalence.

The pipeline is

\[
\text{polytopes and dissections}
\longrightarrow
\text{Tits complex}
\longrightarrow
\text{virtual bundle}
\longrightarrow
\text{Thom spectrum}.
\]

That makes ordinary stable tools available: homology of Thom spectra, equivariant homology, spectral sequences, group homology, and infinite-loop-space methods.

## 4. Group-homology reduction

The Thom-spectrum identification yields

\[
H_m(K(\mathcal P_G^X))
\cong
H_m(G;Pt(X)^t).
\]

Rationally,

\[
K_m(\mathcal P_G^X)\otimes\mathbb Q
\cong
H_m(G;Pt(X)^t)\otimes\mathbb Q.
\]

For Euclidean \(X=E^n\), when \(G\) contains all translations, the relevant groups are already rational and the group-homology expression holds integrally.

This is the higher analogue of the classical coinvariants description in degree zero. It moves the hard part of higher scissors congruence into a recognizable algebraic problem: compute group homology with coefficients in the polytope/Steinberg module.

## 5. One-dimensional calculation

The one-dimensional cases are completely computable.

For the Euclidean line or circle with orientation-preserving isometries,

\[
K_n\cong \mathbb R^{\wedge(n+1)}.
\]

For the full isometry group,

\[
K_{2n}\cong\mathbb R^{\wedge(2n+1)},
\qquad
K_{2n+1}=0.
\]

The degree-one case connects to interval-exchange transformations and the Sah–Arnoux–Fathi invariant.

## 6. Scissors automorphism groups

Kupers–Lemann–Malkiewich–Miller–Sroka study \(\operatorname{Aut}(P)\), the group of scissors automorphisms of a polytope \(P\): cut \(P\), rearrange pieces by allowed transformations, and reassemble \(P\).

Under their hypotheses, the homology is independent of the chosen nonempty polytope:

\[
H_*(\operatorname{Aut}(P))
\cong
H_*(\operatorname{Aut}(Q)).
\]

The common stable homology is described by the base component of the infinite loop space of scissors-congruence K-theory:

\[
H_*(\operatorname{Aut}(P))
\cong
H_*(\Omega^\infty_0 K(X)).
\]

The proof imports homological-stability machinery of Oscar Randal-Williams and Nathalie Wahl. This gives a group-theoretic interpretation of the higher scissors-congruence spectrum: its infinite loop space controls stable homology of cut-and-paste symmetry groups.

This is a useful bridge to Sarah Yeakel's work on operads with homological stability: the technical machines differ, but both turn stability of geometric symmetry into infinite-loop-space structure.

## 7. The 2026 Solomon–Tits sequel

Alexander Kupers, Ezekiel Lemann, Cary Malkiewich, Jeremy Miller, and Robin J. Sroka posted [*Scissors automorphism groups II: Solomon–Tits theorems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00541) in May 2026.

The classical Solomon–Tits theorem concerns the realization of the poset of proper nontrivial subspaces of a finite-dimensional vector space. The sequel proves variants for collections of geodesic subspaces in Euclidean, hyperbolic, and spherical geometries when the collection is generated by points or by hyperplanes.

This is the geometric-combinatorial input needed to turn the abstract stability theorem from Part I into calculations for particular scissors-automorphism groups. Part I describes a planned third paper, *Scissors automorphism groups III: Homology calculations*. No public arXiv record for that title was located in the 2026-10-09 check, so these notes treat it as announced rather than inspected.

## 7. Relation to the shared trace paper

Bohmann–Gerhardt–Malkiewich–Merling–Zakharevich construct trace maps on higher scissors-congruence groups. One input used in Malkiewich's Thom-spectrum paper is their homotopy-orbit comparison

\[
K(\mathcal P_1^X)_{hG}\simeq K(\mathcal P_G^X).
\]

This is the clearest direct reason to read the Malkiewich and Bohmann notebooks together. The former identifies the spectrum geometrically; the shared trace work studies structured maps out of it.

## 10. Worked picture: what is “higher” about a dissection?

Take a square and cut it by one diagonal. Degree zero says only

\[
[\square]=[\triangle_1]+[\triangle_2].
\]

Now choose a second dissection, say a vertical cut. Both decompositions represent the same square, but they are related by refinements. Refining both cuts simultaneously produces a common subdivision. A higher K-theory model does not throw away this web of refinements: chains of refinements, symmetries between them, and coherences between chains become simplices in a space, and stabilization packages that space into a spectrum.

The assembler construction therefore retains the topology of the whole cut-and-paste category instead of adding a higher invariant by hand.

## 11. Open problems and promising directions

- Compute higher groups in dimensions where even \(K_0\) remains difficult.
- Make the Tits-complex/Steinberg-module model genuinely visual: flags, apartments, cuts, and group actions are all drawable.
- Compare higher Dehn invariants with the Campbell–Zakharevich refinement of Goncharov's conjectural picture.
- Follow the scissors-automorphism interpretation far enough to see explicit generators and relations rather than only stable homology.
- Compare the Thom-spectrum model with Malkiewich's parametrized-spectra technology. Both turn geometric local data into stable objects, but they do so for different input categories.
